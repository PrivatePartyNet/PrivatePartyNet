How PrivatePartyNet works

PrivatePartyNet is a private place for a group of friends to chat, talk, share screens and play LAN games together. Each person can make a server of their own, which their PC runs; others find it on the network or join with an invite, and everyone on the server can chat, talk and share screens. There is nothing to install besides the app and no account to make.

Finding your way around

Everything is in one window, and there is only ever one: starting PrivatePartyNet again brings that window to the front. Down its left edge is a rail: Home at the top, then your own server once you have made one (marked with a party hat), then every server you joined, then a + for making your server or joining another, and Settings at the foot. A server stays on the rail for good, whether it is open or not, until you leave it. Click one to show it. A short white bar beside a server means messages you have not read, and a red number means people waiting to be let in. A dimmed server is off or out of reach; its place says so, with what you can do (start it, try again, read its saved messages, or leave it). A server stays connected while another one, or Home, is shown, so a call goes on: its Mute, Deafen and Leave buttons then wait at the foot of the rail, with the server's button marked with a speaker. Right-click a server you joined to leave it for good (you are asked once more), or your own to copy an invite, stop it or start it. About and How open over the window; Escape closes them.

Home has a list of pages on the left: Home (you and your picture, your server, and who is asking to join), Join a server, Nearby PCs, Game network, Direct messages, Saved chats and Settings. A number beside Home is how many people are waiting to be let in, and a green light beside Game network means one is in use. In a narrow window the list shows only its icons.

A server's room is laid out the way Discord users expect. On the left are the server's name and icon (click them for the server menu), its channels, the voice controls when you are in a voice channel, and your name. The middle shows the channel you picked: a text channel's chat, or a voice channel's screens. On the right are the people waiting to join, your own live screens, and the members. The bar along the bottom shows the connections. When the window is narrow the members slide over the middle, and below that the channels open from the button at the top left.

Your own server

On Home, give your server a name and press Create your server. It goes first on your rail and stays there. Your PC runs it: it starts with PrivatePartyNet (untick Start my server when PrivatePartyNet opens to stop that), and Stop server on Home turns it off without taking it off anyone's list. Rename it on Home, or from Server settings in its own menu, where the owner and the admins also give it an icon (a PNG, JPEG, GIF or WebP picture up to 2 MB, shown as it is). Your PC announces it on the network (UDP port 8766), and it listens on the port in the port box (8765 unless you change it). The first time, Windows may ask whether to allow the app through the firewall. Allow it on private networks.

Joining a server

On the Nearby PCs page, pick a PC that shows "server open" and press Ask to join. PCs on a VPN you share, such as Radmin VPN, show up here too; such a server asks for its password, and a box for it appears under the list. The host sees your request and presses Let in. A request that nobody answers within 30 seconds expires. When you are let in, the server goes on your rail for good and opens there, in the one window. Each time PrivatePartyNet starts, it reaches every server on your list again by itself, and someone let in before comes straight back in with no one having to say yes. A host on 3.2.0 or older cannot open inside the one window; its place then says it did not open here. The link that lets you in works once and expires after two minutes.

Inside a server's room

The bar at the top names the channel you are looking at. For a voice channel it also has Go Live, with an arrow beside it for Stream settings, then View options and Fullscreen. The last button hides the members, to give the middle the whole window, and the choice is remembered. A server has room for up to 16 people. To leave a server, open its menu (its name, top left) and choose Leave server, or right-click it on the rail.

Chat belongs to its text channel: click a text channel and its messages fill the middle. Someone who joins later reads the earlier messages in the channel, and every message is saved, encrypted, on the host's PC. When the host's PC is off, the server cannot be opened, though its place on the rail leads to the messages saved on yours. Guests can read the chat but not post.

Sharing your screen

A screen is shared inside a voice channel, and only the people in that voice channel see it. Join one first (Go Live outside a voice channel only says so). Leaving the channel, or moving to another, ends what you were sharing there. Open Stream settings (the arrow beside Go Live), pick a screen (screens are numbered from left to right, and keep their number), and press Go Live. To share another screen as well, pick it and press Go Live again. Each person can share up to four screens at once, and each live screen is listed under Your screens with its own Stop button.

PrivatePartyNet captures your screen at the size the people watching need: when everyone has it in a small tile, it captures and sends a smaller picture, which uses far less processor time, and when someone makes it big or goes fullscreen it goes back to full size within a couple of seconds. Quality sets the largest picture you send, and Frame rate sets the speed you aim for. A change here applies to your live screens at once; to change one screen only, use the settings button on its tile.

Mouse pointer decides whether your pointer is in the shared picture. It is on by default. The screen capture leaves the pointer out, so PrivatePartyNet draws it onto each frame, including the text cursor, the hand and the busy ring.

PrivatePartyNet captures a screen through the graphics card (Windows Desktop Duplication) when it can. On the PC this was tested on, an ultrawide screen (3440 by 1440) was captured at about 57 frames a second with 60 selected, and 30 selected gave 30. If the graphics card or its driver cannot do that, PrivatePartyNet falls back to a slower capture of about 30 frames a second. What other PCs receive also depends on the network and on their processors; the bar at the bottom shows the rate each connection reaches. A screen that is not changing sends a picture twice a second.

Watching screens

The screens of your voice channel show when you look at that channel. A voice channel with someone sharing is marked LIVE beside their name in the channel list. Looking at a voice channel you are not in shows who is there and a button to join it (or move to it), never its screens. While you look at a text channel, or another server, screens are not received at all, which saves both sides' connections; looking back starts them again within a second or two.

Every live screen is a tile named for its owner and the screen, such as "Alex - Screen 2". Screens start in the grid view, where each tile keeps its own shape, so an ultrawide screen is not squeezed into a box.

In the grid view you can drag a tile to move it and drag its edges or corners to resize it. A tile snaps to the edge of the stage, to the middle of the stage, and to the edges and middles of other tiles, and takes the size of a nearby tile when you come close; thin lines show what it is lining up with. Resizing keeps the picture's shape. Hold Shift to resize freely. Reset layout (in View options) hands the arranging back to the grid, and so does changing Receive size.

Click a tile to make that screen big. The other screens shrink to previews in a row along the bottom. Click a preview to make it the big one, click the big screen or press Escape to go back to the grid.

Right-click any screen, including your own, for a menu. "Don't show this stream" turns it into a black tile that says the stream is hidden and has a link to show it again. For a screen from someone else, hiding also tells them to stop sending it to you, which saves their upload and your download until you show it again. Hiding your own screen only hides your preview. The menu also makes a screen big.

Point at a screen and two small buttons appear in its top right corner: settings and fullscreen. Each one affects that screen and no other. On someone else's screen, settings sets the receive size and upscale for that screen alone, over the choices in View options. On your own screen, settings sets that screen's quality, frame rate and mouse pointer. Fullscreen makes the window cover your whole display with that one screen on it: the bars and the rail step aside and the stream fills the display. Press the button again, or Escape, to come back. The Fullscreen button in the top bar does the same for the whole room. Screens never open in windows of their own, not even a floating picture-in-picture one: PrivatePartyNet is one window.

Receive size (in View options) sets how big each picture is. Fit my window fills the stage and follows your window. The 100%, 75%, 50% and 25% choices show the picture at that share of the sender's own size. The sender is asked for a picture of that size too, which saves their upload.

Upscale, when on, draws each received picture smoothly at the size of its tile. This uses your processor, and the figure in View options shows what it costs right now. Previews in the strip are not upscaled.

The bar at the bottom

The bar at the bottom of a server's room starts with a CPU chip: the share of this PC's whole processor used by PrivatePartyNet and its browser engine, updated every second. After it comes one chip for each person you are connected to. A colored dot shows the state of the connection, and the chip shows only the numbers that apply: a green down arrow with what you receive, an amber up arrow with what you send, a purple frame icon with the frames a second, and a blue corner icon with the picture size. A chip for someone who is not sending or receiving anything is just their name.

Hover a name for the details: each screen in each direction with its rate, frame rate, size and the share of the PC spent encoding or decoding it (an estimate from the codec's own timing), whether the encoder is being limited by this PC's processor or the network, the ping, the packet loss and the jitter. When there are too many names for the bar, every chip gives up detail first, and the last few names collapse into a "+" count.

The members list shows each person's ping beside their name, updated every 2 seconds, with a dot: green under 80 ms, yellow from 80 ms, red from 150 ms or when more than 5% of what is sent is lost, and grey when it is not known yet. The number is the ping between you and them, measured on your own connection to them. Without one, it is their ping to the host, which the host measures every 2 seconds. Your own row shows your ping to the host, and the host's own row shows none. When someone's connection is shaky it says "unstable" or "reconnecting…" in amber instead. Hover a ping for the jitter, the packet loss, the way the connection goes (for example Direct over IPv6, or through a member's PC) and where the number comes from. The people in a voice channel have the same dot in the channel list, and the number on their cards when you look at the channel. The chips in the bar at the bottom use the same numbers and colours. While your own connection to a server is unstable or reconnecting, its place on the rail has an amber dot.

Settings

Settings (the gear at the foot of the rail) sets the appearance: Dark (the default), Light or Match Windows, which changes the whole window at once, every server's room included. It also sets your own accent color (a swatch, the R, G and B sliders or a hex code; blue unless you pick another), the text size (90 to 130 percent) and the spacing (Compact, Default or Spacious). The whole window follows those three, the servers you join included; they are kept on this PC and nobody else sees them. Settings also turns automatic updates and test versions on or off, checks for updates, opens the data folder, shows your identity, and opens this page and the About page.

Updates

With Automatic updates on (the default), PrivatePartyNet asks GitHub a few minutes after it starts, and every six hours, whether there is a newer version, downloads it in the background and checks it. A banner at the top of Home then says it is ready: Restart now installs it and starts the app again, and Later installs it when you next close the app. It never restarts while your server runs, during a call or while the game network is on; it says it is waiting instead. With Automatic updates off, nothing is asked of GitHub until you press Check for updates (on the Settings page or the About page); a newer version then offers Update, Later or Skip this version. Include test versions (on by default) also offers versions that are still being tried out.

An update is the newest release of PrivatePartyNet on GitHub. The app installs a file only when its size and fingerprint (SHA-256) are the ones GitHub publishes for it, and only from a version newer than the one running. An installed copy runs the new installer for you, with its progress showing; a portable copy unpacks the new version beside its folder and swaps the two once it has closed. Your settings, chats and keys stay where they are.

Servers, channels and roles

A server keeps its name, icon, channels, roles and chat between runs. The left side of the room lists the channels: a hash sign marks a text channel and a speaker a voice channel. Click a text channel to read it; a yellow number beside a channel is how many messages you have not read. To stop a busy channel counting, right-click it or use its Mute button: it is dimmed, marked with a crossed-out bell and shows no number, while its messages still arrive and show when you open it. Mutes are yours alone, kept on this PC for each server; Unmute, or a second right-click, brings the number back. Clicking a voice channel's name shows the channel (who is in it, and its screens once you are in it) and never joins it, so a stray click never opens your microphone; its Join button is the way in, and the people in it are listed under it with a green ring on whoever is talking and LIVE beside whoever is sharing. Scroll to the top of a channel to load older messages. The owner and the admins add, rename and delete channels (a deleted channel's messages stay saved). Roles are Owner, Admin, Member and Guest. A guest reads the chat, and may join a voice channel to watch its screens and listen, but cannot post, talk or share. The member list on the right shows who is online and who is away, each with their picture. The owner and admins change roles and remove people from the three dots beside a name; the owner makes admins.

Pictures, files and voice

Drag pictures and files onto a server's room, anywhere in it, or paste a picture into the message box. While you drag, the room says which channel they go to. Up to ten go at once, one message each. A picture (PNG, JPEG, GIF or WebP) is sent at its own size, up to 12 MB. It is saved again first, which removes what the file recorded about where and when it was taken, and a large one is saved at a slightly lower quality if it needs to fit. Its dimensions are never reduced: a picture that still does not fit in 12 MB is sent as a file instead. Direct messages take pictures the same way.

Any other file is sent as it is, with a card showing its progress and a Stop button. The host's PC keeps it, encrypted, and everyone sees a card with its name, its kind and its size. Save asks where to put it; a file in the chat is never opened in the window. Members' PCs keep the chat but not the files themselves, so a file can be saved while its host is on.

Under the server's name, the room shows how much the host keeps: "Storage 3.4 GB of 20 GB" with a bar, or, with no cap, how much room is left on the host's disk. The owner and the admins set two limits there with File limits: the largest file, and all files together. Each is No limit (the start) or a size, and when both are set, the cap is at least ten times the largest file. Pictures count too. A file over a limit is stopped before anything is sent, with the reason. The host's disk always keeps half a gigabyte free.

Point at a message to see its delete button: your own messages, and anyone's for the owner and admins. A deleted message goes for everyone, and its picture or file goes from the host's PC too once no other message uses it. Copies already saved on members' PCs stay theirs. To talk, press Join beside a voice channel (six people at most). You hear only the people in the same voice channel, and they hear only you. While you are in voice, a green band at the bottom left says Voice connected and names the channel, with Mute, Deafen, Settings (microphone, speakers, push to talk, a volume for each person) and Leave. Mute and Deafen turn red and change their words while they are on. Push to talk works with the window in front: hold V when it is on.

Your picture (Home, You, Choose a picture) shows beside your name on every server you are in: in the member list, beside your messages and in voice. It is a PNG, JPEG, GIF or WebP up to 2 MB, kept as it is and shown in a circle. Your app hands it to each server when you open it there, and the server keeps it for next time.

Sounds

Short sounds play when you join or leave a voice channel, when someone joins or leaves yours, when you mute, unmute or deafen, when a message arrives while you are not looking at it (never from a muted channel), and when someone asks to join your server. Settings, Sounds has the volume and a switch for each group, with Play to hear it. They are public-domain sounds (see About).

Direct messages

The Direct messages page of Home is where you write to anyone on your network, with no server needed. Add a person from Nearby; the first message from a stranger shows them as New, and a message still waiting to reach someone shows as "to send" beside their name. Messages are sealed so only the other person can read them, and your copy stays on your PC. A message to someone who is away waits and goes when they are seen again. Safety number shows twelve groups of digits: if both of you see the same number, nobody is in between.

Joining from outside your network

Press Copy invite on Home and send the code to your friend; it contains your server's password. The code also shows in a box under the button, in case copying is refused. They paste it on the Join a server page and press Ask to join, and you see their request on Home and at the top of the room's right panel. Once in, your server stays on their list. Home also shows, in short labels, how people outside your network can reach you (an open router port, IPv6, carrier-grade NAT). Where the router allows it, PrivatePartyNet opens the port by itself. When nothing can reach your PC directly (an internet connection that shares one public address between many customers, such as Starlink, with no IPv6), the invite still works: the two PCs find each other's public address through the BitTorrent network's shared address book (signed records anyone can store and nobody can change), then both send at the same moment, which opens each router for the other. Where both PCs have IPv6, they try it first (IPv4 starts a quarter of a second later), which also opens a firewall such as Starlink's. This takes up to two minutes the first time: the host looks for new joiners every few seconds, and the shared address book is slow to answer. Both PCs should run the same, newest version; an older host notices joiners too slowly for this to work. When both routers give a new port for every destination, that cannot work, and the invite goes through a relay instead (see below), if your group has one. Without a relay the app says so, and IPv6 or a forwarded port on either side fixes it.

Relays, for routers that let nothing through

Members reach each other the most direct way that works: the same network, IPv6, a port the router opened, or both PCs sending at once through their routers. IPv6 comes before every IPv4 way except the same network: it has no shared addresses, so it gets past carrier-grade NAT, and a game still sees its ordinary network address while what carries it goes over IPv6. The bar at the bottom of the room and Home say "Direct over IPv6" when that is the way. Some pairs of routers cannot be crossed that way. Then the two can go through a relay, which passes on what they send without being able to read it, since it is encrypted before it leaves their PCs. A connection on a relay keeps checking for a direct way and moves to it as soon as one opens.

There are two kinds. A member whose PC others can reach can turn on Let members who can't reach each other go through this PC, under Game network. It is off until you turn it on, because it uses your upload; you choose the most it may use, and Home shows who is going through your PC now. The group can also run a relay server of its own on a small rented server (the relay folder of the source says how). The owner enters its address, secret and certificate fingerprint under Relay server on Home, once. The secret stays on the owner's PC: members get credentials that last six hours and are renewed while they are on the server, and the relay server also carries voice and screens when nothing else can. When a member cannot be reached at all, Home says who and why.

When the internet drops out

Short dropouts are normal on Starlink and similar links, and they do not throw anyone out of a server. Someone whose connection drops keeps their place, their role, their voice channel and their screens for two minutes, and nobody sees them leave and come back. Their room says Reconnecting, with the seconds counting up, and its place on the rail says reconnecting; the room finds its way back by itself, even if their PC got a new address or moved to another Wi-Fi. Nobody has to let them in again, and the chat they missed arrives. Voice and screens pick up again without starting over, and screens are sent smaller while a connection is struggling. Other people see that person's screens marked Reconnecting meanwhile. The game network stays on through a dropout and uses a relay meanwhile if there is one. If the connection does not come back within two minutes, the room says so and offers Rejoin. All of this needs PrivatePartyNet 3.5 or later on both PCs; with an older version on either side, a dropout works as it did before.

The game network

Each server is also a small private network for games that play over a LAN. On the Game network page of Home, press Use for games beside a server. Windows asks for permission to add a network adapter named PrivatePartyNet, and this PC gets that server's game address, shown beside the server with a green light and on its own line. The page also lists the other members on that network, each with their name, their game address, how this PC reaches them and the game network's own ping to them, with the same coloured dot as the members list. Your address on a server stays the same every time, and the host is always the address ending in .1. Start the game and use that network; a game that looks for other players on the network finds the people in that server. Game packets go from your PC straight to the player they are for, sealed with WireGuard, and not through the host. When two PCs cannot reach each other directly, the packets take the same way round that the app finds for everything else, still sealed, so nothing in between can read them. Someone who leaves, is removed or is banned is cut off by everyone at once. Only one server's game network is in use at a time. Only game traffic for that server goes over it: it is never a way to the internet or into anyone's home network. Windows marks the adapter's network Private. Broadcasts and multicast, which many games use to find each other, reach every member. The adapter's packets are at most 1270 bytes, so that one still fits when it goes through a relay over IPv6. Turning it off, closing PrivatePartyNet, or leaving the server removes the adapter. Windows' own services on your PC, such as file sharing and Remote Desktop, are closed to members; under What members of your servers can reach on this PC you can open each group, for this PC only. Games are not affected either way.

Your saved chats

Chats, pictures and contacts are kept encrypted in the data folder, with a key that Windows protects for your sign-in. Under Saved chats you can add a password (a forgotten password cannot be recovered), and export everything to one file sealed with a passphrase, or import such a file. Importing adds what is missing and changes nothing already here. Include my identity only to move to a new PC: anyone with that file and passphrase can then be you.

Where your files are

Settings, your server's certificate, the log and the rooms' saved choices (a webview folder: receive size, pointer, member list) are in the data folder, %LOCALAPPDATA%\PrivatePartyNet. Open it from Settings, Open data folder. Uninstalling asks whether to delete it, and keeps it if you say no. A silent uninstall always keeps it.

Limits

  • Two PCs that both sit behind routers which give a new port for every destination reach each other only through a relay: a member who turned relaying on, or the group's relay server. Without one the app says so.
  • Voice channels hold six people at most, because each person's audio goes straight to every other person.
  • Files in direct messages are not supported yet; send them on a server. A file is kept on its host's PC only, so it can be saved while that PC is on.
  • The installer is not signed, so Windows may show an unknown publisher.
  • Each server's certificate is made on its host's PC. PrivatePartyNet windows accept it without a warning.

If something does not work

  • Nobody shows on the Nearby PCs page: check that both PCs are on the same network, that neither is on a VPN, and that the firewall allows the app on private networks.
  • Ask to join says there was no answer: the host's server has to be running.
  • Go Live says to join a voice channel: screens are shared inside a voice channel; press Join beside one first.
  • A screen does not appear: stop it and press Go Live again. The log file (see the About page) records what went wrong, and every ten seconds while you share it notes the frame rate your window actually drew.
  • A screen looks black: it may be hidden. Right-click it, or press Show hidden streams in View options.
